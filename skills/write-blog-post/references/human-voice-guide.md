# Human Voice Guide (Anti-AI Prose)

Catch the patterns that make writing *sound* AI-generated, even when the ideas are good. Read this before drafting or editing anything in a real person's voice.

Pairs with:
- `style-guide.md`: house rules (em-dash ban, heading case, naming).
- `oreilly-word-list.md`: preferred spelling and term conventions.
- `llm-cliche-patterns.md`: the exhaustive phrase catalog. This guide covers the **structural tells** a word list misses.

The single best test: **read it out loud. Would the author say this to one person across a table?** If it sounds like a caption, a TED talk, or a LinkedIn thought leader, pull it back.

---

## AI Tropes to Kill

These structures are the most common reason a draft "sounds like AI" with zero banned words in it.

### 1. The clipped antithesis ("Not X. It's Y.")
The biggest tell. A short negation fragment, then the "real" answer.

- ❌ "Not because it's easy. Because most of it is just being a mirror."
- ❌ "It's not about the tools. It's about the mindset."
- ❌ "This isn't a setback. It's a setup."

Fix: write one honest sentence, or cut the negation. Real writers rarely set up a fake opposite just to knock it down.
- ✅ "Most of coaching is just being a mirror, honestly."

### 2. The rule-of-three triad (anaphora)
Three parallel fragments in a row, often with a repeated opener. Humans use it once in a while for real emphasis. AI uses it as default cadence.

- ❌ "A friend works. Rubber-ducking works. Even talking to your dog works."
- ❌ "So can a friend. So can a coworker. So can a stranger."

Fix: dissolve into one flowing sentence, vary the structure, or demote one item to a parenthetical.
- ✅ "A good friend, a coworker you'd never think to ask, even talking to yourself on camera (which, yes, is what I'm doing right now)."

### 3. The tidy aphoristic closer (the mic drop)
Ending on a neat, balanced, fortune-cookie line that resolves everything.

- ❌ "You're just making space to hear what you already know."
- ❌ "Sometimes the answer was inside you all along."
- ❌ "Growth isn't comfortable, but it's worth it."

Fix: end on something specific, self-aware, or genuinely open: a real question, an admission, a next step, a detail.

### 4. "X isn't about Y, it's about Z."
Cousin of #1. Reframing via a stated-then-disclaimed binary.

- ❌ "Pricing isn't about numbers, it's about psychology."

Fix: say the thing you mean without the scaffolding.

### 5. Overworked metaphor hand-holding
- ❌ "Think of your business like a garden you have to tend…"
- ❌ "Your website is a house, and the foundation is…"

Fix: use concrete, lived detail instead of assigned analogies. If a metaphor shows up, it should be one the author would actually reach for, dropped in casually, not explained for three sentences. (A single well-chosen analogy for a genuinely complex concept is fine. Extended, explained metaphor is the tell.)

### 6. Cliché openers and connectors
- Openers: "In today's fast-paced world," "Let's talk about," "Imagine a world where," "Here's the thing."
- Connectors: "That being said," "At its core," "To put it simply," "It's worth noting that," "Furthermore," "Moreover."
- Closers: "At the end of the day," "The bottom line is," "In conclusion," "When all is said and done."

Fix: cut them. Start with the actual thought.

### 7. Em-dash overuse
The #1 punctuation tell. **No em-dashes (—).** Use a period, a comma, parentheses, a colon, or an ellipsis for a trailing, run-on thought.

### 8. Empty intensifiers and hedges-as-filler
"truly," "incredibly," "absolutely," "definitely," "really," "very," "simply," "essentially," "ultimately." Delete, or replace with something specific.

## The Author's Real Voice (Do These)

Don't over-correct into something flat. Stripping tells is half the job; the other half is sounding like a specific person. These are the general markers of a human writer. The author's own markers belong in their voice profile (see `voice-profile-template.md`).

- **Lived, specific detail.** Real numbers, real moments, real objects. Specificity is the opposite of AI vagueness. **Never invent it.** If the draft needs a detail the author hasn't given you, leave a `[DETAIL: ...]` placeholder and ask. A fabricated anecdote is worse than a generic sentence.
- **Parentheticals and asides.** Thinking out loud in parentheses reads human. Keep them.
- **A little wander in the middle.** It's okay for a thought to take a detour before it lands. AI is suspiciously tidy.
- **First person and contractions** where the register allows it (see the contractions rule in `style-guide.md`).
- **Opinions framed as observation.** "I've been noticing," "This surprised me," "I could be wrong, but," "I keep coming back to this."
- **Honest uncertainty.** "I haven't figured this out." "I don't have a clean answer yet." Name the tension without forcing a resolution.
- **Endings that invite, not conclude.** A question, an admission, or a concrete next step beats a polished summary line.
- **The author's own tics.** Pet phrases, running jokes, casual interjections. Pull these from the voice profile and the corpus. Casual words that are genuinely the author's ("honestly," "and that's fine") stay, even if they appear on a generic filler list. The voice profile overrides the generic lists.

## Self-Check Before Delivering

1. Read it aloud. Does any line sound like a slogan instead of a sentence?
2. Scan for the clipped antithesis ("Not X. Y.") and the three-fragment triad. These two slip through most.
3. Is the ending a tidy aphorism? Rewrite it into something specific or open.
4. Any em-dashes? Replace them.
5. Concrete, lived details, or floating in the abstract? (Real details only. Placeholders where you don't have them.)
6. Would the author actually say this to one person? If it feels like performance, pull it back.
